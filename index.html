The origin

Where streamers meet spin rods

Long before it was a business, it was just a soft-baiting habit — hours spent on New Zealand's rivers and lakes, going through packet after packet as plastic tails tore off on good fish or vanished on a snag. Around the same time, a university paper on mitigating environmental impact got me thinking there had to be a better way to get that same lifelike swimming action. That better way is built by hand from wool, knit yarn, and other sustainable materials instead of PVC, tied to hold up through fish after fish instead of needing to be replaced every session.

Why it matters

Fewer plastics in the river

Soft plastic lures tear, bite off, and get lost on snags far more often than most anglers admit, and once they're gone, they don't just disappear. Research published in 2024 has already shown that these lures leach phthalates and dozens of other persistent chemicals into the water they're lost in, sometimes for weeks at a time. That's not to say you should panic every time you open a tackle box.

The aim behind Charged Streamers isn't to cause hate against those (legends) who choose to soft bait. It's a reason to ask whether there's a better way to fish the same water.

Phthalates are a well-studied class of endocrine disruptors — meaning they interfere with hormone systems rather than causing damage through simple toxicity alone. In fish, laboratory research has linked this kind of exposure to feminization of males, intersex conditions where testes develop egg cells, reduced fertility, altered sex ratios, and lower survival rates in developing embryos and larvae. Over several seasons, that can mean fewer fish reaching adulthood, even if the fish being caught still look completely healthy.

The 2024 lure study didn't stop at measuring chemical concentrations. Researchers tested whether leachate from the lures could trigger a hormonal response in living cells — and one extract did, behaving like a female hormone in testing. That's not proof of a direct line between lost soft plastics and a declining fish population in any specific river; that kind of field evidence takes years to establish. But it does place the chemicals leaching out of lost lures in the same category already known to affect how fish reproduce.

Freshwater fisheries in New Zealand depend on water quality and habitat that most anglers don't think twice about while they're casting. Every lure left in a river is a small, cumulative choice about what condition that water is in for the fish living in it, and for whoever's fishing it next season, and the season after that. Changing how we gear up now is a genuinely practical way to keep that water, and those fish, in the kind of shape worth coming back for.

This isn't about guilt-tripping anyone over a packet of soft plastics in the garage. It's about recognising that small changes in what we tie on the end of our line add up across an entire angling community, and that the water systems producing the best fishing conditions in the future are the ones we look after today.

Process

How each one gets tied

Weight & hook

Each head is poured and painted around a hand-selected hook, weighted for the way we want it to swim.

Thread base

A tight thread base locks the anchor point before any material goes on.

Layer the tail

Wool, knit scraps, and fabric are wound and layered in stages for a tapered profile.

Finish & inspect

Every jig gets whip-finished, checked for balance, and given a wiggle test before it ships.
